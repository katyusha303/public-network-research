# BACKGROUND

## How WiFi works
A Wi-Fi network is identified by two key names: the **ESSID** (Extended Service Set Identifier), which is the human-readable network name users see when connecting (e.g., "CoffeeShop WiFi"), and the **BSSID** (Basic Service Set Identifier), which is the unique MAC address of the specific access point broadcasting that network. Since multiple access points can share the same ESSID to extend coverage, the BSSID distinguishes each physical device. Wi-Fi operates on **channels** — specific frequency ranges within the 2.4 GHz or 5 GHz bands — and devices must be tuned to the same channel to communicate. When a client connects to a secured network, it performs the **4-way handshake**, a process where the client and access point exchange four EAPOL (Extensible Authentication Protocol over LAN) messages to prove they both know the network's password without ever transmitting it directly. This handshake generates encryption keys used for the session, and it is the moment an attacker targets when attempting to capture a WPA/WPA2 hash for offline cracking.

## Encryption Basics: WPA2 vs WPA3, HTTP vs HTTPS, and PMF
Wi-Fi networks rely on encryption protocols to protect data in transit. **WPA2** (Wi-Fi Protected Access 2) has been the dominant standard for over a decade, using AES encryption and a 4-way handshake to derive session keys. However, WPA2 has a critical weakness: its handshake is vulnerable to offline dictionary attacks, and its management frames (like deauthentication packets) are unencrypted, meaning an attacker can forge them to forcibly disconnect clients. **WPA3**, introduced in 2018, replaces the pre-shared key handshake with **SAE** (Simultaneous Authentication of Equals), which is resistant to offline dictionary attacks and provides forward secrecy. On the application layer, **HTTP** (Hypertext Transfer Protocol) transmits data in plaintext, making it trivial for an attacker on the same network to read passwords, cookies, and form data. **HTTPS** (HTTP Secure) wraps HTTP in TLS encryption, ensuring that even if traffic is intercepted, it remains unreadable. Finally, **PMF** (Protected Management Frames), defined in the **802.11w** amendment, cryptographically protects management frames such as deauthentication and disassociation. Without PMF, an attacker can spoof these frames to disconnect clients at will — a core step in the Evil Twin attack chain. PMF is mandatory in WPA3 and optional in WPA2, and its absence is one of the primary reasons public Wi-Fi remains vulnerable to forced disconnection attacks.

## Threat Model: Who attacks public networks and why
Public networks attract attackers with different skill levels and motives. **Script kiddies** are low-skilled individuals who use pre-built tools for curiosity or bragging rights. **Cybercriminals** are financially motivated and represent the most common threat — they steal credentials for resale, commit banking fraud, or deploy ransomware at scale. **Advanced Persistent Threats (APTs)** are state-sponsored groups targeting specific individuals like journalists or executives for espionage. While APTs are rare on public Wi-Fi, they often use the same techniques (Evil Twin, deauth, captive portals) as low-level attackers.

## The attack to be experimented : The Evil Twin Attack
An **Evil Twin** attack is a type of Wi-Fi phishing where an attacker creates a fake access point that mimics a legitimate network's **ESSID** (name). Because client devices remember networks by name — not by BSSID — they will automatically attempt to reconnect to the attacker's AP if it broadcasts a familiar SSID with a stronger signal. The attack typically begins with a **deauthentication** flood, which forcibly disconnects victims from the real network by spoofing management frames. Once disconnected, victims see the fake AP with the same name and connect to it. From there, the attacker controls the entire network path: they can serve a **captive portal** (a fake login page) to harvest credentials, perform **DNS spoofing** to redirect victims to malicious sites, or use **ARP spoofing** to intercept all traffic in a Man-in-the-Middle (MITM) position. The Evil Twin is particularly dangerous because it exploits trust in a network name rather than breaking encryption — no password cracking is required. Its success depends on two factors: the victim's device auto-connecting to a remembered SSID, and the absence of **PMF (802.11w)** , which would otherwise protect against forged deauthentication frames.
